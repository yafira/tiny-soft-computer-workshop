about the workshop

what actually makes something a computer?

most people assume a computer means a screen, a keyboard, a rectangle you stare at. this workshop starts by taking that apart.

a basic definition

any device that can:

  1. store instructions, a program
  2. execute those instructions in sequence
  3. process input, even if the only input is "power turns on", into output

by this definition, a thermostat, a calculator, a player piano, and your tiny soft computer all count. they vary in generality, not in whether they're really computers. a laptop is general-purpose, it can run different programs and do different things. your kit runs one small program, and was never meant to run anything else. that's not a lesser kind of computer. it's just a more honest one, one you can actually see all the way through.

references

two pieces that shaped how we think about this project.

the embroidered computer

irene posch + ebru kurbak, part of the stitching worlds project

a working 8-bit electromechanical computer made entirely from gold, linen, hematite, wood, silver, and copper thread. it functions as a computer and as a decorative textile at the same time, using embroidered relays the way pre-semiconductor computers once did. no conventional electronic components at all.

it also carries a real historical thread worth knowing: ada lovelace once observed that the analytical engine "weaves algebraic patterns, just as the jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves." the jacquard loom, with its binary punch-card storage, is widely considered a predecessor to the modern computer. textiles and computing share real ancestry, this workshop continues that lineage, it doesn't invent it.

1-bit symphony

tristan perich

a cd jewel case with a microchip and speaker wired directly onto the sleeve, wiring exposed, nothing hidden. the chip is programmed with a full 40-minute symphony, compressed onto a single 8kb chip, performed live in real time every time it's switched on. it's not a recording. it's computation, stripped down to almost nothing, with the workings left in plain sight.

one soft and hidden, one bare and exposed, both are legitimate answers to the same question. your enclosure doesn't have to look like either. this is one answer, what's yours?
